# Meeting Prep

Ten minutes before the meeting, not an hour of homework. You
walk in knowing what the meeting is for, what you need from it,
and what you will say first. That is the whole job of prep.

This is the before-meeting skill on purpose. It does not clean up
notes after the meeting. That is a different job, and it has its
own skill. This one only gets you ready to walk in.

## Who this is for

Small business owners, realtors, teachers, contractors, anyone
who sits down with a client, a supplier, or a boss and does not
want to wing it. No jargon, no slide deck required. A phone note
works fine.

## The 10 minute routine

### 1. The outcome in one sentence (2 minutes)

Write one sentence that finishes this way:

"When this meeting ends, I will have ____."

Not "a good chat" and not "talk about the project." A real
outcome. Examples:

- "When this meeting ends, I will have a yes or no on the start date."
- "When this meeting ends, I will have the price range the client can approve."
- "When this meeting ends, I will have the three dates they are free next week."

If you cannot write that sentence, that is useful information.
You are not ready for a meeting, you are ready for a quick message
asking what the meeting is supposed to decide. Send that message
instead of sitting through an hour with no point.

### 2. Three things you must know (4 minutes)

Answer these three, short answers only:

- **What do they want?** One line, in their words if you can. "They want the kitchen done before Thanksgiving" beats "discuss renovation timeline."
- **What do I need from them?** One decision, one number, or one date. If you need three things, pick the one that blocks everything else and lead with it.
- **What could go wrong?** One honest line. "They might push the price down" or "They might bring someone new who has not seen the plan." Naming it now means it does not surprise you in the room.

Then gather only what those answers point to. One number, one
photo, one document, one past message. If you are pulling five
files, you are over-prepping. Stop at what your three answers
actually need.

### 3. Your first line and your one ask (4 minutes)

Write down two things before you walk in or dial in:

- **Your first line.** The first thing you will say, in plain
words. "Thanks for making time, I want to leave today with a start
date we both agree on." Starting strong beats warming up for ten
minutes while everyone wonders why they are there.
- **Your one ask, and your one line you will not cross.** The ask
is the thing from step 2 that you need most. The line is what you
will not agree to on the spot, so you do not say yes under
pressure. "I can give you a date today, but I will not lock a price
until I check material costs." Saying that to yourself beforehand
is what keeps a good meeting from turning into a bad promise.

Then write the whole brief in this shape, and keep it where you
can glance at it during the meeting:

```
Meeting: [who, when]
Outcome I want: [one sentence from step 1]
They want: [one line]
I need from them: [one decision, number, or date]
Watch out for: [one line]
My first line: [plain words]
My one ask: [the thing I need most]
I will not agree on the spot to: [one line]
Bringing: [one number, photo, document, or message, at most]
```

That is the whole skill. Ten minutes, one note, and you stop
being the person who leaves a meeting thinking of what they
should have said.

## What this skill does not do

It does not write your meeting notes, it does not chase follow
ups after the meeting, and it does not prep you for a high stakes
negotiation, a legal matter, or a hiring decision. Those deserve
more time and, for the serious ones, a real person advising you.
This skill is for the everyday meetings that eat your week when
you walk in cold.
